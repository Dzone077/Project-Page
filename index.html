Abstract

This thesis develops reinforcement learning-based locomotion for the Booster T1, a humanoid robot whose long, heavy arms and dexterous hands give it an asymmetric morphology that the official controller does not account for, resulting in a short, stompy gait with the arms held retracted. The core of the work is natural and robust movement. A legged controller is first trained and deployed on the real robot, and then extended to the whole body, with the arms swinging in anti-phase with the legs. Compared with the official controller, the deployed whole-body policy reduces mean mechanical power by 41% and torque-rate RMS by 29.5%, and a second formulation that minimises the cost of transport shows that a restrained arm swing is the economical solution for this morphology.

Walking is then enhanced with reference motions retargeted from an established motion library and our own recordings, organised by speed so that the gait is expressive at high velocities and reserved as it slows down. This policy walks with a less crouched posture and converges in roughly 65% fewer training steps. Finally, a perceptive climbing policy observes a heightmap of the terrain ahead and learns ascent and descent as structured sub-movements, climbing risers of up to 25 cm in simulation, and is blended with the walking policy at deployment so that neither behaviour degrades and switching remains smooth. Every stage follows the same training, validation and deployment pipeline, and is replicated on six further state-of-the-art humanoid robots.

Structure of the Work

Objective 1

Legged Locomotion

The first controller drives only the legs, with the arms held either retracted, as in the official controller, or rested closer to the ground. Keeping the arms out of the problem lets the policy focus on the gait itself. It transfers directly onto the real robot, with joint trajectories closely matching those in MuJoCo and a wider, more fluid range of movement than the official Booster controller. Lowering the arms, even while still fixed, already reduces the load the legs have to take.

Objective 2

Whole-Body Locomotion

Extending the policy to the whole body shows that, for this morphology, the arms are an asset rather than a burden. Left unconstrained, the policy first raised its heavy arms into balance postures, so admissible windows on the shoulder angles keep the swing natural. With the arms swinging in anti-phase with the legs, the deployed policy reduces mean mechanical power by 41% and torque-rate RMS by 29.5% relative to the official controller, and the arm trajectories transfer to the real robot with correlations of 0.95–0.99.

A second formulation that makes the cost of transport the primary objective converges to the same restrained swing, indicating that it is the economical solution for the T1, reached through a more flexed leg posture.

Sim-to-Real and Simulation Validation

Energy Efficiency and Morphology

Trained under the same energy-efficient objective, each robot is compared at the speed that matches the T1's at equal Froude number, so the remaining differences come from morphology and learned behaviour. The economical swing follows each morphology rather than a universal pattern: robots with more human-like proportions swing their arms visibly more and stay upright, whereas the T1, with long and heavy arms, rotates its shoulder little yet displaces about as much arm mass as the K1. Efficiency alone therefore does not lead the T1 to a human-like gait, which motivates the next objective.

Objective 3

Reference Motion Fine-Tuning

Reference motions supply what the reward function could not. Walking is retargeted from an established motion library and from our own motion-capture recordings with an interaction-mesh retargeter built on OmniRetarget. Its implementation is not morphology-agnostic: the human targets are scaled by a constant fitted to the Unitree G1, so on the 1.20 m T1, roughly 29% shorter than the average human, it over-scales the targets by about 10%. The retargeting was adapted per robot, and the resulting walking cycles are organised by velocity into a motion library.

The resulting gait is expressive at high speeds and reserved as the robot slows down, with strong, coordinated arm motion, a less crouched leg posture and lower ankle effort. On the T1, reference-guided training also stabilises in roughly 65% fewer steps.

Objective 4

Perceptive Stair Traversal

A climbing policy observes a heightmap of the terrain ahead, built from the head camera, and decomposes stair traversal into the sub-movements a person performs instead of stumbling into the riser and recovering. The T1 ascends full flights reliably up to 25 cm risers in validation, well beyond the 18 cm regulated range, with a limit that is consistent across tread depths and simulators and therefore reflects the robot's physical reach. Descent reaches the same robustness except on the shallowest treads, where the feet leave almost no margin for placement.

At deployment the climber is blended with the reference-motion walker, preserving both behaviours with smooth hand-offs and without either expert degenerating.

Structured Sub-Movements

Alternating Stride and Live Perception

The stair behaviours are validated in simulation, including the live camera-to-heightmap conversion and the deployment script, but not yet on the real robot.

Across every objective

Seven Humanoids, One Pipeline

Every training and validation environment has a counterpart for six additional robots: the Unitree G1, Booster K1, Fourier GR3, AgiBot A3, PAL Robotics Kangaroo and Deep Robotics DR02. They test whether the methods generalise across stature and morphology: the shorter K1, the human-like proportions of the G1, the greater height and weight of the GR3, A3 and DR02, and the Kangaroo's parallel leg linkage. On stairs, the reachable heights follow leg length.
