Reinforcement learning locomotion for the Booster T1: legged and whole-body walking deployed on the real robot, a natural gait learned from human motion capture, and perceptive height traversal, all replicated on six further humanoids.

Abstract

This thesis develops reinforcement learning-based locomotion for the Booster T1, a humanoid robot whose long, heavy arms and dexterous hands give it an asymmetric morphology that the official controller does not account for, resulting in a short, stompy gait with the arms held retracted. The work centers on natural and robust movement. A legged controller is first trained and deployed on the real robot, and then extended to the whole body, with the arms swinging in anti-phase with the legs. Compared with the official controller, the deployed whole-body policy reduces mean mechanical power by 41% and torque-rate RMS by 29.5%. A second formulation that minimizes the cost of transport shows that a restrained arm swing is the economical solution for this morphology.

Walking is then enhanced with reference motions retargeted from an established motion library and our own recordings of human locomotion, organized by speed so that the gait is more pronounced at high velocities and restrained as it slows down. This policy walks with a less crouched posture and converges in roughly 65% fewer training steps. Finally, the framework is extended to perceptive height traversal: a policy that observes a heightmap of the terrain ahead learns ascent and descent as structured sub-movements and, in simulation, climbs steps of up to 25 cm. It is blended with the walking policy so that neither behavior degrades and transitions remain smooth. Every stage follows the same training and validation pipeline, and is replicated on six further state-of-the-art humanoid robots.

Policy Structure and Learning

Every policy is a deterministic actor \(\mu_\phi(s) = a\). It observes the joystick command \((v_x, v_y, \omega_z)\), the joint positions and velocities, the IMU's angular velocity and gravity vector, a gait clock \((\cos 2\pi f t, \sin 2\pi f t)\) and its previous action, and outputs joint position offsets around the home pose.

Training follows an Asymmetric Actor-Critic: the critic additionally sees privileged quantities only a simulator can provide, such as the base linear velocity and height, body accelerations, push forces and feet velocities, while the actor keeps to what the real robot can measure.

The policies are trained with TD3, run over thousands of parallel simulated environments as in FastTD3. Two critics are learned against a shared target that takes the smaller of their estimates, with clipped noise on the target action:

\[ y_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma \min_{i=1,2} Q_{\theta_i'}\big(s_{t+1}, \tilde a(s_{t+1})\big), \qquad \tilde a(s_{t+1}) = \operatorname{clip}\big(\mu_{\phi'}(s_{t+1}) + \epsilon',\, a_{\min},\, a_{\max}\big), \]

and the actor ascends the critic, \(\nabla_\phi J = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \mathcal{D}}\big[\nabla_\phi Q_\theta(s, \mu_\phi(s))\big]\), with target networks following by Polyak averaging, \(\theta' \leftarrow \rho\,\theta' + (1-\rho)\,\theta\).

Objective 1

Legged Locomotion

The first controller drives only the legs, with the arms held either retracted, as in the official controller, or rested closer to the ground. Keeping the arms out of the problem lets the policy focus on the gait itself. It transfers directly onto the real robot, with joint trajectories closely matching those in MuJoCo and a wider, more fluid range of movement than the official Booster controller. Lowering the arms, even while still fixed, already reduces the load the legs have to take.

In Simulation

Developed Policy vs. Official Booster Controller

Over the same forward walk, the developed policy moves through a much wider range than the official controller: 2.1× at the hip (47.5° against 22.5°), 1.4× at the knee and 1.8× at the ankle, while every pitch joint stays at least 15° from its actuator limits.

It is also more repeatable. Measured as the deviation between time-normalized strides relative to each joint's range, it drops from 12% to 7.5% at the hip, 10% to 7.7% at the knee and 21% to 12% at the ankle. The policy walks at 1 Hz against the Booster's 1.9 Hz, taking fewer and longer steps.

Sim-to-Real Gap

No simulator models the real world exactly, and though the two are hard to tell apart in motion, the joints show where they differ. The curve shapes and the gait frequency are kept, and the hip and knee pitch follow their simulated trajectories closely.

The ankle, the joint most affected by contact with the ground, departs the most. The hip ranges match within 2°, while on the robot the knee works about 8–10° more and the ankle about 10° less than in simulation.

Objective 2

Whole-Body Locomotion

Extending the policy to the whole body shows that, for this morphology, the arms are an asset rather than a burden. Left unconstrained, the policy first raised its heavy arms into balance postures, so admissible windows on the shoulder angles keep the swing natural. With the arms swinging in anti-phase with the legs, the deployed policy reduces mean mechanical power by 41% and torque-rate RMS by 29.5% relative to the official controller, and the arm trajectories transfer to the real robot with correlations of 0.95–0.99.

A second formulation that makes the Cost of Transport the primary objective converges to the same restrained swing, indicating that it is the economical solution for the T1, reached through a more flexed leg posture.

Moving the upper body keeps the main stepping pattern but changes the lower-body configuration within it. The hip-pitch trajectories stay closely aligned, while the knees and ankles differ: knee flexion is reduced over large parts of the cycle, particularly on the right leg, and the ankle pitch shifts without losing its periodic structure.

The recordings also show some left–right asymmetry, more pronounced in the now-active upper body, since symmetry is not enforced in training. This is one of the motivations for the reference motions that follow.

Coordinating the Arms

Anti-phase arm swing does not emerge on its own, so arm timing was made a precondition of an existing reward: the feet-swing reward is granted only when the upper limbs follow the stepping pattern. During the swing of leg \(a\), the shoulder of the opposite arm \(\bar a\) has to pitch in the same direction as the swinging hip,

\[ \mathbb{1}^{(a)}_{\text{limbs follow}} = \mathbb{1}\big[\,\dot q^{\,\text{sh}}_{\bar a}\;\dot q^{\,\text{hip}}_{a} > 0\,\big], \qquad a \in \{\text{L}, \text{R}\}, \]

so a step taken with uncoordinated arms earns nothing. Since the condition compares directions rather than prescribing one, it holds for forward and backward walking alike.

Torque and Power

The shoulder pitch develops recurring torque peaks with the added arm motion, but this extra demand is small next to the reductions at the hip pitch, hip yaw and knee pitch. Over the three-lap route, mean absolute mechanical power falls from 91.3 W to 53.7 W (−41.2%), positive power by 40.3% and torque RMS by 16.4%.

The motion is also smoother: torque-rate RMS drops by 29.5% and angular-jerk RMS by 80.7%, so the lower demand does not come from a more irregular gait.

Sim-to-Real and Simulation Validation

The trained motion transfers directly onto the real robot. Against the average of Isaac Sim and MuJoCo under the same command, the cycle-averaged trajectories of the shared joints differ by an RMSE of only 1.8°, and the mean posture of every joint agrees within 1.5°.

The legs transfer almost exactly, with a correlation of 0.99 and an RMSE of 6% of their range for hip and knee pitch; shoulder and elbow correlate at 0.95–0.99, and torque RMS and positive power differ by only 9% and 10%.

Energy Efficiency and Morphology

The second formulation makes the Cost of Transport the primary objective, so the arms are rewarded only through their effect on the work done by the legs. The per-step cost uses positive mechanical power,

\[ \mathrm{CoT} = \frac{E}{m\,g\,d} = \frac{P}{m\,g\,v}, \qquad \mathrm{CoT}_t = \min\!\left(\frac{\sum_j \max(\tau_j \dot q_j,\, 0)}{m\,g\,\max(|v_x|,\, v_{\min})},\; c_{\max}\right), \]

with \(v_{\min} = 0.15\) m/s to avoid the singularity near standstill and \(c_{\max} = 3\), about five times the cost of a normal gait, so that a stumble cannot dominate the reward.

To compare robots of different sizes, each is run at the speed that matches the T1's at equal Froude Number, the ratio of inertial to gravitational forces over the leg length \(L\):

\[ \mathrm{Fr} = \frac{v^2}{g\,L} \approx 0.04. \]

Trained under the same Energy-Efficient objective and compared at equal Froude Number, the remaining differences come from morphology and learned behaviour. The economical swing follows each morphology rather than a universal pattern: robots with more human-like proportions swing their arms visibly more and stay upright, whereas the T1, with long and heavy arms, rotates its shoulder little yet displaces about as much arm mass as the K1.

Efficiency alone therefore does not lead the T1 to a human-like gait, which motivates the next objective.

Objective 3

Reference Motion Fine-Tuning

Reference motions supply what the reward function could not. Walking is retargeted from an established motion library and from our own motion-capture recordings with an interaction-mesh retargeter built on OmniRetarget. Its implementation is not morphology-agnostic: the human targets are scaled by a constant fitted to the Unitree G1, so on the 1.20 m T1, roughly 29% shorter than the average human, it over-scales the targets by about 10%. The retargeting was adapted per robot, and the resulting walking cycles are organised by velocity into a motion library.

The resulting gait is expressive at high speeds and reserved as the robot slows down, with strong, coordinated arm motion, a less crouched leg posture and lower ankle effort. On the T1, reference-guided training also stabilises in roughly 65% fewer steps.

Retargeting for Non-Human Proportions

Each frame is retargeted by an interaction-mesh optimization: the Laplacian coordinates \(L(\cdot)\) of the human keypoints are matched on the robot, with a smoothness term, a soft term that draws the contacting soles flat onto the ground and a hard constraint that keeps the feet apart,

\[ \begin{aligned} q_t^\star = \operatorname*{argmin}_{q_t}\quad & \underbrace{\sum_i \big\lVert L(p^{\text{source}}_{t,i}) - L(p^{\text{target}}_{t,i}(q_t)) \big\rVert^2 + \lVert q_t - q_{t-1} \rVert_Q^2}_{\text{Reference}} + \underbrace{w_g \sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}_t} s_{t,i}\,\big(e_z^\top f_i(q_t) - \bar z_{t,i}\big)^2}_{\text{Sole contact}} \\ \text{subject to}\quad & \text{the original OmniRetarget constraints}, \qquad \underbrace{n(q_t)^\top\big(f_l(q_t) - f_r(q_t)\big) \ge d_{\min}}_{\text{Lateral foot separation}} , \end{aligned} \]

where \(f_i(q_t)\) is a sole keypoint, \(\mathcal{C}_t\) the keypoints in contact and \(n(q_t)\) the trunk's lateral axis projected on the ground.

Velocity-Conditioned Tracking

All walking cycles are normalized to a common length of \(N = 49\) frames, which fixes one gait frequency for the whole library, \(f_{\text{ref}} = 50\ \text{Hz} / N \approx 1.02\ \text{Hz}\). Each cycle keeps its captured speed \(\bar v_i\) as a label of its style, and at every step the tracked cycle is the one closest to the commanded planar speed:

\[ i^\ast = \arg\min_i \big|\, \bar v_i - \lVert \mathbf{c}_{xy} \rVert \,\big| . \]

Because every cycle shares one phase clock, a new command changes the style and stride of the reference but never breaks the rhythm of the gait. Imitation is then rewarded separately for the arms and the legs, so the error of one group cannot saturate the other's learning signal:

\[ r_G = \mathbb{1}_{\lVert \mathbf{c} \rVert > 0.1}\cdot \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{\sigma_G} \sum_{j \in G} w_j\,(q_j - \hat q_j)^2\Big), \qquad G \in \{\text{Arms}, \text{Legs}\}. \]

On the Real Robot

The Legged Policy holds the arms near their fixed pose, yet its arm trajectories are the most irregular: holding them against gravity and the reactions of walking forces constant small corrections. Freeing the arms removes most of this, but the whole-body swing stays conservative, mostly in the low elbow peaks.

With reference guidance, shoulder and elbow follow strong, smooth and coordinated waves, and the movement becomes far more symmetric: left–right waveform similarity across the arm joints rises from 46.8% to 95.3%, with the elbow going from 51.2% to 98.1%.

On the Six Other Robots

Objective 4

Perceptive Height Traversal

The Climbing Policy observes a heightmap of the terrain ahead, built from the head camera, and decomposes stair traversal into the sub-movements a person performs instead of stumbling into the step and recovering. The T1 ascends full flights reliably up to 25 cm steps in validation, well beyond the 18 cm regulated range, with a limit that is consistent across tread depths and simulators and therefore reflects the robot's physical reach. Descent reaches the same robustness except on the shallowest treads, where the feet leave almost no margin for placement.

At deployment the climber is blended with the Reference Motion Policy through a gate driven by the heightmap, preserving both behaviours with smooth hand-offs and without either expert degenerating.

Why a Perceptive Policy

The walking policy becomes unstable on steps above about 4 cm. Simply adding stairs to its training does not help: however strongly velocity tracking is weighted, the policy learns to stop once pressed against the first step. Rewards that actively induce climbing do work with a blind, proprioceptive-only observation, but they reproduce the known blind strategy: the robot only detects a step once a foot strikes it, so it stumbles into the step and relies on a learned recovery reflex instead of planning where to place the foot.

That reflex becomes more dangerous as the step grows taller relative to the robot, and the T1 is short: an 18 cm regulation step is, in proportion, a person stepping onto 36 cm steps. The climbing policy developed here therefore perceives the terrain ahead, and its rewards follow the sequence in which a person climbs: seeing the step, placing the stance foot at a suitable distance, lifting the swing foot over the height and transferring weight onto the tread.

Perceiving the Terrain

The climber observes a \(5 \times 5\) heightmap in its own heading frame, spanning 0.1–0.7 m ahead of the base and \(\pm 0.3\) m to either side. Each sample is taken relative to the terrain directly under the base, so on flat ground the map is identically zero instead of oscillating with the gait, and positive values are capped at the next step height:

\[ \mathrm{hm}_{ij} = \operatorname{clip}\Big(\min\big(z_{\text{terrain}}(\mathbf{p}_{ij}) - z_{\text{terrain}}(\mathbf{p}_{\text{base}}),\; h_{\text{next}}\big),\, -1,\, 1\Big). \]

The walking rewards that would resist a tall step are not removed but released gradually by a continuous Step Engagement \(w \in [0, 1]\), ramped over a small height band from the highest point of the two nearest rows, so the flat-ground gait is untouched away from the stairs. Near a step, the gait clock also slows down, to 75% cadence on small steps and 50% on tall ones:

\[ w_{\text{asc}} = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{h_{\text{near}} - 0.005}{0.018 - 0.005},\, 0,\, 1\Big), \qquad w = \max(w_{\text{asc}}, w_{\text{desc}}), \qquad \dot\phi = 2\pi f_{\text{ref}}\,\big(1 - s(h)\,w\big). \]

Structured Sub-Movements

Each sub-movement is shaped by its own term. Positioning, for instance, rewards every touchdown by the distance \(d\) from the center of the sole to the next step, with a target that moves back on taller steps to leave room for the swing arc:

\[ r_{\text{foothold}} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(d - d^\ast)^2}{\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad d^\ast = \frac{\ell_f}{2} + \delta + k\,\max(0,\, h - h_0), \]

where \(\ell_f\) is the foot length and \(\delta\) the desired toe-to-step gap. It is paid only if the foot was airborne and moved closer to the step, so tapping in place earns nothing.

Blending Walking and Climbing

Rather than asking one network to learn both behaviours, the Reference Motion Policy and the climber run side by side and their actions are mixed by a gate \(\beta\) computed from the heightmap, so that both a step up and a drop engage the climber:

\[ \mathbf{a} = \beta\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{walk}} + (1 - \beta)\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{climb}}, \qquad \beta^{\text{inst}} = 1 - \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{\max|\mathrm{hm}| - 0.02}{0.05 - 0.02},\, 0,\, 1\Big). \]

The gate has hysteresis. It follows \(\beta^{\text{inst}}\) down immediately, so control passes to the climber as soon as a step comes into view, but returns towards the walker by at most \(1/40\) per control step, 0.8 s at 50 Hz:

\[ \beta_t = \begin{cases} \beta^{\text{inst}}_t, & \beta^{\text{inst}}_t < \beta_{t-1},\\[0.3em] \min\!\big(\beta^{\text{inst}}_t,\; \beta_{t-1} + \tfrac{1}{40}\big), & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]

so a flickering heightmap, as when the last step leaves view, cannot make control bounce between the two. Blending in action space is well posed because both policies share an action space, a home pose and a phase clock, and the climber is warm-started from the walker, so their actions agree near the switch.

At the bottom of a flight, control is handed back to the walker over a blend of about 2.8 s. Within a further second and a half the uneven height-traversal gait gives way to the regular, alternating cycles of the reference gait, at about one second per cycle, without the robot stopping or moving erratically.

Alternating Stride and Live Perception

The height traversal behaviours are validated in simulation, including the live camera-to-heightmap conversion and the deployment script, but not yet on the real robot.

Across Every Objective

Cross-Embodiment

Every training and validation environment has a counterpart for six additional robots: the Unitree G1, Booster K1, Fourier GR3, AgiBot A3, PAL Robotics Kangaroo and Deep Robotics DR02. They test whether the methods generalise across stature and morphology: the shorter K1, the human-like proportions of the G1, the greater height and weight of the GR3, A3 and DR02, and the Kangaroo's parallel leg linkage. On stairs, the reachable heights follow leg length.

Height Traversal Across Robots

Climbing the same flight, all six robots converge to the same basic strategy: the lead leg flexes strongly at the hip and knee at the start of each step, while the trailing leg follows later in the cycle with a smaller excursion. What differs is the effort. For the smaller T1 and K1 a 15 cm step is large in proportion to their legs, and their lead hip reaches the highest flexion of the group, whereas the taller A3 and GR3 clear the same step with roughly half of that excursion. Most robots climb at a similar pace, but the K1 needs more than twice as long, taking extra footsteps on each tread to reposition before the next step.

The robots with longer legs and easier clearance are robust over wider ranges of step height. The GR3, DR02 and A3 decay gradually, staying above 90% success up to roughly 25 cm (A3), 26 cm (GR3) and 28 cm (DR02), whereas the T1 goes through the same drop within about 2 cm. The K1 struggles from 21 cm, as expected of its stature, yet climbs whole flights of steps taller than 23% of its height. The Kangaroo's parallel linkage gives it a leg reach close to the T1's despite its size.
